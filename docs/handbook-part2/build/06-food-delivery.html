Part B · System design (HLD)

A food delivery platform across many cities

Three apps (customer, restaurant, delivery partner) and one order passing between them. Order volume is moderate. The heavy traffic is location: hundreds of thousands of partners reporting GPS every few seconds, and customers watching them move.

What the numbers tell you

Orders are a normal transactional workload (hundreds per second). Location is a streaming workload 250× bigger, and it must never touch the orders database. That split is the backbone of the design: a transactional order path and a separate in-memory, lossy, high-throughput location path.

H5. High-level design

{{fd-arch}}

Two planes. The order plane (gateway → order service → Postgres → Kafka) is transactional. The location plane (realtime gateway → location service → Redis GEO → tracking fan-out) is high-volume and is allowed to lose a ping. Dispatch joins the two.

  • Cells by city. Each city (or a group of small ones) has its own order database shard, Redis GEO and dispatch workers. Orders and partners rarely cross cities, so one city's outage doesn't spread.
  • Order plane: every transition is UPDATE … WHERE id AND status = expected, plus an outbox row. Restaurants and partners get pushes. Notifications consume events.
  • Location plane: partners keep a persistent connection. Pings are validated (speed and jump filters), written to Redis GEO with a TTL (a stale partner simply disappears), and streamed to Kafka for history and ETA training.
  • Tracking: when an order has a partner, the tracking service subscribes to that partner's position channel and forwards updates to the customer's socket through the realtime gateway.
{{fd-seq}}

The dispatcher never writes order state directly. It asks the order service to assign, and the conditional update guarantees one partner per order.

H7. Detailed component design

1 · Location ingestion

  • Partners send batched pings over a persistent connection (MQTT or WebSocket). That's cheaper on battery and bandwidth than an HTTP request every 4 s.
  • The location service drops implausible points (a jump of 2 km in 4 s) and writes GEOADD geo:{city} lng lat partnerId and HSET partner:{id} lastSeen …. A sweeper runs ZREM on partners not seen for 30 s.
  • 75k writes a second across around 20 city shards is ~4k/s per Redis, which is easy. Pings also go to Kafka (partitioned by partner) for history.
  • The alternative is H3 or geohash cells (cell → set of partners), which let you query neighbouring cells at fixed resolutions and shard by cell. Redis GEO uses geohash internally anyway.

2 · Dispatch

  • Trigger: OrderAccepted, timed so the partner arrives as the food is ready: start searching at about prepTime − travelTime.
  • Candidates: GEOSEARCH within 3 km, filtered to status = FREE. Score each by travel ETA (from the ETA service), how well its arrival matches the ready time, and fairness (time since the partner's last order).
  • Batching: dispatch collects orders for 10–30 s and solves the assignment for the whole batch (minimum total cost, for example the Hungarian algorithm) instead of greedily one at a time. That's F2 in the LLD.
  • No double offers: SET partner:{id}:offer orderId NX PX 30000 before offering. Only one dispatcher can hold a partner, and the key expires on its own if the partner ignores the offer.
  • Accept: the order service runs UPDATE orders SET partner_id = $p, version = version + 1 WHERE id = $o AND partner_id IS NULL. If 0 rows change, someone else won, and the partner is released.

3 · Waiting queue and reassignment

An order with no candidates stays in a per-city queue (the orders_waiting_partner index), and dispatch retries it every 10 s with a wider radius and a bonus incentive. This is the LLD's dispatchWaiting, run on a schedule. A partner who goes offline mid-order triggers reassignment from the current position of the food (at the restaurant, or with the partner, which needs ops to step in).

4 · Live tracking fan-out

  • When an order has a partner, the tracking service subscribes to loc:{partnerId} (Redis pub/sub, published by the location service). Each update is forwarded to the order's channel at the realtime gateway, which pushes it to the customer's socket.
  • Throttle to one update every 5 s per order, and send the ETA only when it changes by more than a minute.
  • 350k sockets need ~10–20 gateway nodes with sticky routing. A node failure means clients reconnect and get a fresh snapshot.

H8. Interviewer questions and counters

Q1Why Redis GEO and not PostGIS?

Answer

75k position updates a second is a write rate that would fill a relational database with dead rows, and the data is only valuable for seconds. Redis keeps the latest position in memory, and radius queries take milliseconds. PostGIS is the right tool for the durable shapes: restaurant delivery zones and city polygons.

Push-back: "Redis dies, and you've lost every partner's location."

For about 4 seconds: the next round of pings rebuilds it. That's exactly why this data can live in a store that doesn't need to be durable. Run it with a replica for faster failover. Dispatch pauses briefly, and the order data is untouched.

Q2How do you stop one partner being assigned two orders at once?

Answer

Two guards. A short-lived offer lock per partner (SET NX PX) stops two dispatchers offering the same partner. A conditional update on the order (WHERE partner_id IS NULL) stops two partners taking the same order. Stacking orders (two orders from one restaurant on one trip) is a deliberate batching decision, not an accident.

Q3Nearest partner first is simple. Why do anything more complex?

Answer

Nearest-first per order is locally optimal and globally poor. It sends the only nearby partner to order A when order B, next door, had nobody else. Batching orders over a short window and minimising total pickup time does measurably better. Also, the "nearest" partner is useless if the food won't be ready for 20 minutes: what matters is arriving close to the ready time.

Push-back: "Batching adds 20 s of delay."

Food takes 15 minutes to prepare, so 20 seconds of dispatch delay is invisible. Start dispatch based on prep time, and batch when the window allows.

Q4When do you charge the customer?

Answer

Authorise (or take payment) before the order reaches the restaurant, so the kitchen never cooks an unpaid order. If the restaurant rejects or nobody can deliver, refund or void the authorisation automatically. Cash on delivery skips the step, and those orders need risk limits per customer. F5 in the LLD has the state details.

Q5WebSocket or polling for tracking?

Answer

WebSocket, or SSE: one connection per live order, pushed every 5 s. Polling every 5 s from 350k clients is 70k requests a second, with each one doing authentication and routing. Push costs memory per connection, but almost no CPU between messages. Keep polling as the fallback for networks that block WebSockets.

Q6The restaurant doesn't respond to a new order.

Answer

Escalate on timers: push again at 60 s, an automated phone call at 2 minutes, auto-cancel and refund at 5 minutes, and mark the restaurant "busy" so it stops getting orders. These timers are scheduled jobs keyed by order id (the job scheduler from Part 1), and each one runs only if the order is still PLACED. The conditional update guarantees that.

Q7Why partition by city? What about orders near city borders?

Answer

Almost every order, restaurant and partner belongs to one city, so a city is a natural failure and scaling boundary. Border areas get assigned to one city's cell. A partner who crosses over moves cell when their status is FREE. Global things (user accounts, payments, restaurant catalogue) live outside the cells.

Q8GPS is noisy: the partner jumps around the map, and ETA flips between 5 and 15 minutes.

Answer

Filter on ingest (drop impossible speeds, weight by reported accuracy), snap to the road network for display, and smooth ETA with hysteresis: only show a change above a threshold that persists for two updates. Positions shown to the customer are presentation, so smoothing them is fine. Dispatch uses the raw latest point.
