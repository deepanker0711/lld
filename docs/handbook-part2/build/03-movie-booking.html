Part B · System design (HLD)

A national ticketing platform

Thousands of screens across hundreds of cities. Browsing is read-heavy and can be cached. Booking is a contention problem: 300 seats, and on opening night 100,000 people who all want the middle of row H.

What the numbers tell you

Average write volume is tiny, and all the seat data fits on one machine. The design is driven by the hottest show: thousands of conflicting writes against 300 rows. Sharding by show_id keeps each show's seats together, so a multi-seat hold is one local transaction.

H5. High-level design

{{mb-arch}}

Browsing is served from search and cache. Show inventory is the only writer of seat state. Seat changes go out as events, and a push service fans them out to everyone viewing that show.

  • Read path: movies and shows come from Elasticsearch behind a CDN. The seat layout is immutable per screen and cached for days. The status bitmap is cached in Redis per show and updated from SeatsChanged events.
  • Write path: holds go to show inventory, which runs one conditional update per request on the show's partition. Booking orchestrates payment and the final state, just like the checkout saga.
  • Hot shows: when demand for a show crosses a threshold, the gateway's waiting room lets users in at a rate the partition can serve (H7.3).
  • Partner cinemas: chains with their own box-office systems sync both ways through an adapter. Counter sales come in as holds from a "box office" channel, so there is still one writer of seat state.
{{mb-seq}}

CONFIRMING fences the hold before money moves. The sweeper skips CONFIRMING seats, so a slow payment can't lose them, and a dead payment can't keep them forever.

H6. Database design

Choice: Postgres, partitioned by show. Seat state needs multi-row atomic updates and constraints. Each show's seats are a few hundred rows that are always accessed together, so show_id is the natural shard key. Catalog data is replicated to Elasticsearch for search.

schema
  • The exclusion constraint is the database form of the LLD's overlap check: two admins scheduling at once can't both succeed.
  • Old shows: when a show has finished, its show_seats rows are archived. Bookings keep the seat list in booking_seats, so history doesn't depend on the live table.

H7. Detailed component design

1 · The hold: one statement, all or nothing

hold

Postgres locks each matching row, so two overlapping holds serialise on the shared seats and the second sees them as taken. Lock order is not a deadlock risk here because each statement locks rows in index order. Holding nothing across a network call keeps lock time to milliseconds.

2 · Hold expiry

  • Correctness is lazy: an expired hold counts as free in the WHERE clause above, so the next hold simply takes the seat. No timer has to fire on time.
  • Cleanliness is a sweeper: every 30 s, UPDATE … SET status = 'FREE' WHERE status = 'HELD' AND hold_expires_at < now(), which emits SeatsChanged so seat maps turn green again.
  • CONFIRMING is skipped by both, until hold_expires_at + payment window. After that, the reconciler asks the PSP before releasing anything.

3 · Hot-show admission (waiting room)

  • Demand per show is measured at the gateway. Above a threshold, new visitors to that show get a signed queue ticket with a position, and an admitter lets in N users a second (for example 200/s), sized to what the partition can serve.
  • Admitted users get a short-lived token that the hold endpoint requires. Without a token, the request gets 429 at the edge and never reaches the database.
  • This also gives fairness: requests are served in arrival order, instead of rewarding whoever retries fastest.

4 · Live seat map fan-out

  • Show inventory publishes SeatsChanged {show, version, seats} through its outbox. A push tier subscribes per show (Redis pub/sub, or a Kafka consumer that routes by show) and forwards to every socket watching that show.
  • Clients apply deltas in version order. On a gap or a reconnect they fetch the snapshot again with GET /seats. This is a classic snapshot-plus-deltas design.
  • For a hot show, batch the updates every 500 ms. 100k viewers × 50 updates a second unbatched is 5M messages a second. Batched, it's 200k.

H8. Interviewer questions and counters

Q1Why lock seats in the database? Redis with SETNX is much faster.

Answer

The database is the source of truth, and a single conditional update is atomic across all the seats with no extra moving parts. Average load is 50 holds a second. Redis (the Lua script in M1) is a valid choice for hot shows. But then you have two stores that must agree when the booking is confirmed, and if Redis loses a key on failover, the same seat can be sold twice.

Push-back: "Opening night, 1.7k holds/s on one show."

Most of those fail fast on a couple of hundred rows, and the waiting room caps the arrival rate anyway. If measurements show the partition still struggles, add Redis as a pre-filter: reject requests for seats Redis already shows as held, and let the database decide the rest.

Q2Optimistic or pessimistic locking?

Answer

The hold statement is effectively optimistic, because the condition is checked in the update itself and nothing is locked while the user thinks. Row locks last only for the duration of the statement. A SELECT … FOR UPDATE followed by application logic and then an update would hold locks across a round trip and serialise everyone on popular seats.

Q3Why 5 minutes for a hold? Why not 15?

Answer

It's a trade-off between letting slow payers finish and keeping seats from being locked by people who walked away. Seats are scarce and demand is spiky, so it's short, unlike a shopping cart. CONFIRMING then extends the hold only for users who actually started paying. Measure abandonment and tune it per channel.

Push-back: "Bots hold seats and let them expire, over and over."

Rate-limit holds per user and device, require a login before holding, cap active holds at one per user per show, and add a short cool-down after an expiry.

Q4Payment succeeds but the seats were already released and resold.

Answer

CONFIRMING is designed to stop this from happening at all. If it still happens (for example a very late webhook after the grace period), the booking becomes FAILED_SEATS_LOST, an automatic refund is issued, and the user is told straight away. Optionally, offer the nearest free seats of the same type. The PSP payment link expiring before the grace period ends is what keeps this rare.

Q5Why not put every booking request on a queue partitioned by show and process them serially?

Answer

It works, and it removes lock contention: a single consumer per show applies holds in order. The costs are a synchronous user action turned into async (the client has to poll or wait for a push to learn whether it got the seats), and queue lag on hot shows when users need an answer within a second. A conditional update gives the same serialisation per row with an immediate answer. I'd use the queue only if the database truly couldn't keep up after the waiting room.

Q6How do you keep the seat map fresh for 100k viewers without hammering the database?

Answer

Viewers never read the database: snapshots come from the Redis bitmap and changes are pushed (H7.4). Holds are the only database writes, and each produces one event, however many people are watching.

Push-back: "WebSockets are expensive. Why not poll?"

Polling every 2 s × 100k viewers is 50k requests a second that mostly return "no change". With ETags they're cheap, and that's an acceptable fallback (and what mobile clients on bad networks use). A push connection is idle almost all the time, which costs less at this scale.

Q7The cinema's own box office sells seats too. Who is the source of truth?

Answer

One system has to own each show's inventory. Usually that's the cinema chain's system, and we are a sales channel with an allocation or a live API. If they own it, our hold becomes a call to their API with a timeout, and we show "pending" when they're slow. If we own it, their counter becomes another client of our hold API. The bad option is two sources syncing after the fact, because the same seat then gets sold twice.
